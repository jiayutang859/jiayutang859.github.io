How antibody frameworks shape predicted binding

Wang Lab · Dept. of Infectious Diseases & Immunology · University of Florida · with Dr. Yiquan Wang · Research Assistant, continuing into my PhD

The questionWhen the CDRs, antigen, and epitope are held fixed, how much does the antibody framework (the scaffold around the binding loops) change what structure-prediction and design tools say about binding?

Why it matters

Frameworks are often treated as a passive scaffold, but they influence CDR conformation and how the heavy and light chains pack together. If prediction and design tools are sensitive to framework choice, that affects how we graft, humanize, and design antibodies, and how far we can trust in-silico results.

Approach

  • Build a reproducible dataset of paired human VH–VL frameworks, parent antibody structures, and epitope definitions, with provenance tracking
  • Graft fixed CDRs onto different frameworks, keeping native antibodies as controls
  • Run large-scale antibody–antigen predictions with AlphaFold 3 / ColabFold on the HiPerGator cluster
  • Evaluate with epitope-masked structural analysis, and benchmark emerging design tools

Tools & methods

  • AlphaFold 3
  • ColabFold
  • RFantibody
  • Germinal
  • Python
  • HiPerGator HPC

Metrics: AF3 confidence, pose and CDR RMSD, epitope recovery, interface contacts, VH–VL orientation.

Current status

In progress. The dataset and evaluation pipeline are built, and I'm running large-scale predictions and early benchmarks of Germinal and RFantibody. Findings are preliminary and not yet published.

What I'm learning

A confident prediction is not the same as a correct one. Native controls and careful metrics matter as much as the model itself. I'm also building the structural intuition this field depends on, one structure at a time.

M.Sc. researchJul 2024 – Jul 2026

Dietary pulses, the gut microbiome, and human health

Dept. of Food Science & Technology · University of Nebraska–Lincoln · with Dr. Edward Deehan

The questionDo different market classes of dietary pulses, beans that look alike on a nutrition label, have distinct effects on the gut microbiome and cardiometabolic health?

Why it matters

Pulses are affordable, widely eaten, and rich in fiber, but they are often studied as a single food group. If specific market classes act differently in the gut, dietary guidance could become more precise, and we would learn more about how the microbiome links diet to health.

Approach

  • Coordinated a 12-week, three-phase randomized crossover clinical trial with Nebraska dry beans (IRB-approved, ClinicalTrials.gov-registered), plus a human biobank study
  • Co-developed an NIH-style protocol and consent materials; authored 4 SOPs and 2 clinical workflows
  • Led 200+ clinic visits; collected stool, blood, 24-hour blood pressure, and dietary data
  • Led a PICOS-based systematic review of pulse intervention trials

Tools & methods

  • Python
  • R

Randomized crossover design, GCP-compliant operations, systematic review methods, statistical analysis.

Current status

Completed as my M.Sc. research. The trial and systematic-review manuscripts are in preparation, and preliminary results were presented at the ASM Missouri Valley Branch meeting and Nebraska Research Days. See presentations.

What I'm learning

Human biology is noisy, and study participants are generous with their time. I learned how design choices determine what a study can conclude, and that good evidence depends on patient, careful operations as much as clever ideas.

AI-assisted research workflows

Started in the Deehan Lab systematic review · now part of my computational work at UF

The questionWhere can AI tools responsibly take over repetitive research work, such as screening literature, extracting data, or running pipelines, and how do we check they're doing it well?

Why it matters

Much of research time goes into necessary but repetitive work. If AI can take some of it on reliably, scientists can spend more time on judgment, design, and interpretation. That only holds if the tools are validated rather than simply trusted.

Approach

  • Screened 1,800+ references with machine-learning tools (ASReview, TERA-Tool)
  • Used a large language model (Gemini) for preliminary data extraction, validated against human reviewers
  • Gave 3 guest lectures on search strategy, PICOS design, and AI/ML tools for screening and extraction
  • Now applying the same habits to reproducible, provenance-tracked computational pipelines

Tools & methods

  • Gemini
  • Claude Code
  • Cursor
  • Python

Current status

An ongoing practice rather than a single project. It began with the systematic review and now shapes how I build and document computational pipelines.

What I'm learning

AI is most useful when it comes with a clear way to check it. Validation, documentation, and knowing when not to automate are part of the skill.
