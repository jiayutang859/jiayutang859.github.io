In brief

I am a PhD trainee in Dr. Yiquan Wang's lab at the University of Florida, working in computational immunology and antibody engineering: how antibody frameworks, binding loops, and epitopes shape predicted antigen recognition, and when those predictions can be trusted. Before this I trained in food science, human nutrition, the gut microbiome, systematic review, and a human clinical trial. Across all of it, I am interested in how biological interactions can be understood, predicted, and eventually engineered.

About

How I arrived at the questions I care about

I did not begin with a plan to work on antibodies, or on computation at all. My path has been a sequence of questions about how biological systems respond to what we do to them, and how much the answer depends on the surrounding context. Each question made the next one clearer.

I started in food science at Northwest A&F University, drawn to how foods and their bioactive components might act on human health. Fermentation experiments taught me to measure an effect against a control and to distrust a number until the assay behind it was understood. They also shifted my attention from the intervention itself toward the body receiving it. I could show what a food did in a tube. I wanted to know what it did in a person.

That question took me to human nutrition and the gut microbiome at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln. Coordinating a clinical trial and leading a systematic review, I kept meeting the same pattern: diet interacts with microbial communities, those communities interact with their host, and several layers of context decide what an outcome looks like. The same intervention produced different responders. I became less interested in any single ingredient and more interested in the interactions themselves.

Those projects also showed me what experiments alone could not reach: enough participants, enough conditions, enough of a biological design space to explore before committing to a measurement. Building a language-model agent to extract data from hundreds of papers, and checking its output against two human reviewers, was my first computational method that had to earn trust the way an assay does. It convinced me that computation could widen the questions I was able to ask, as long as it was validated like any other method.

That is what led me to the Wang Lab at the University of Florida, and to computational immunology and antibody engineering. Antibody–antigen recognition is a more mechanistic system than a diet and a microbiome. The objects are defined, the interaction is measurable, and a prediction can be tested against a known structure. It is a good place to learn how biological interactions can be understood, predicted, validated, and eventually engineered.

My long-term interest is not one molecule, disease, or experimental system. It is a transferable way of thinking about biological interactions: observe them, understand the objects and the context involved, model and predict the factors that shape them, validate those factors experimentally, and learn to modulate or engineer the interaction. My research subjects changed. The questions kept converging.

Past · breadth

Observe and understand

  1. Observe · Measuring what a food does

    2022 – 2023
    Northwest A&F University · with Dr. Chunxia Xiao

    Food science, for me, was chemistry, microbiology, and a first real question: how does what we eat act on the body? In Dr. Xiao's lab I fermented proso millet with three lactic acid bacteria and measured changes in phenolic profile, antioxidant activity, and starch digestibility. It was my first experience of a biological effect that only existed relative to a control, of replicates that disagreed, and of an assay that had to be trusted before its number could be. Two co-authored papers came out of that work.

    What it left me with: measurement discipline, and the habit of asking what an assay is actually reporting.

    Why I moved on: I could show what a food did in a tube. I wanted to know what it did in a person, and that meant studying the system receiving the intervention.

  2. Understand · Interactions, context, and human variability

    2023 – 2026
    University of Nebraska–Lincoln · with Dr. Edward Deehan

    Clinical nutrition put food, microbes, metabolism, and human health inside one framework. As a master's student I coordinated a 12-week randomized crossover trial of dry bean market classes and the gut microbiome: co-writing the protocol and consent, authoring SOPs, running more than 200 clinic visits, and handling microbiome, blood, blood-pressure, and dietary data from consent form to analysis. I also led a systematic review of 1,800+ records, screened by two reviewers, and built a language-model agent to extract the data, with every value verified by two people.

    The microbiome taught me that phenotypes are produced by interacting communities, that the same intervention produces different responders, and that context decides the outcome. A statistics minor and the review taught me to think in effect sizes and heterogeneity rather than in single results. This is where my interest settled on interactions rather than ingredients.

    What it left me with: design determines conclusions; confounding, selection, and adherence are real; data provenance is a scientific issue; biological behaviour is interaction-driven.

    Why I moved on: the trial and the review also showed me what experiments alone could not reach. Enough people, enough conditions, enough of the design space. I needed methods that could explore before I measured.

Current · depth

Predict and design

  1. Predict · Modeling molecular recognition

    2026 – now
    Wang Lab · Dept. of Infectious Diseases & Immunology · with Dr. Yiquan Wang

    Dr. Wang's approach to AI-assisted biomedical research matched the conclusion I had reached, and I joined his lab. My work is computational immunology: how antibody framework, binding loops, and epitope geometry interact in predicted antigen recognition, and when structure-prediction outputs are reliable enough to act on. I treat a prediction as an assay output, with native structures as controls, replicate runs, and provenance for every input.

    Antibodies are a deliberate choice rather than a change of heart. The objects are defined and the interaction is measurable, which makes them a good system for learning how to model biological interactions at all.

    What I am building: structural intuition, pipeline engineering on an HPC cluster, and the habit of treating a model output as evidence with uncertainty.

  2. Modulate & engineer · From prediction to proposal

    developing

    The same project benchmarks generative antibody design tools against the controls used for prediction, asking which proposed molecules would be worth testing and by what criteria. This is the stage I have the least experience in, and the one the PhD is for.

    Where this points: closing the loop, so that designs return to experimental evidence and the evidence returns as new observations.

How I approach research

One loop, learned one stage at a time

The homepage states the cycle. This is where it came from: each step is a habit I picked up in a particular project, and each one exists because the others do. The centre stays fixed. The cycle keeps turning.

  1. Observe experimental rigor

    Measure a real interaction, against a control, with provenance for every value.

    Where it came from: fermentation assays at NWAFU, then 200+ clinic visits with samples at every phase of a crossover trial.

  2. Understand systems, across scales

    Identify the objects, the context, and the environment that decide the outcome.

    Where it came from: the gut microbiome, where the same fiber produced different responders, and a systematic review that made heterogeneity impossible to ignore.

  3. Model & predict question first, uncertainty attached

    Represent the interaction and predict which factors matter, reporting spread rather than a single answer.

    Where it is now: replicate AlphaFold 3 predictions of one set of binding loops across human frameworks, reported as distributions.

  4. Validate computation earns trust like an assay

    Return predicted factors to experimental or biological evidence before acting on them.

    Where it came from: a language-model extraction agent checked by two human reviewers; now, native antibody structures as controls for every prediction.

  5. Modulate & engineer the stage I am learning

    Improve the interaction by modulating or engineering the validated factors.

    Where it is now: benchmarking generative antibody design tools against the same controls, to decide which proposals are worth testing.

  6. Back to observe

    A designed molecule, like a changed diet, is a new system to observe. The loop is a process, not a destination.

My earlier training sits on the observe, understand, and validate side of this loop. The PhD is adding depth in modeling, prediction, and design. Computation and AI appear here as methods that widen what I can ask, not as the point. The point is the interactions, and learning to run the whole cycle on them.
