About

Why I approach computational biology this way

The question that has followed me from a food chemistry bench to a clinic to a computing cluster is a simple one: how do the things a body meets, whether a nutrient, a microbe, or a molecule, actually act on it, and how would we know?

I am a PhD trainee in Dr. Yiquan Wang's lab at the University of Florida. Before computation I did experimental research: fermentation chemistry, a human clinical trial, a systematic review, gut microbiome work. This page is about how those stages built breadth, how the PhD is building depth, and why that combination shapes the way I use models.

Research development

Past · breadth

Observe and understand

  1. Observe · Measuring biological effects

    2022 – 2023
    Northwest A&F University · with Dr. Chunxia Xiao

    Food science is often mistaken for culinary training. For me it was an introduction to chemistry, microbiology, and the question of how what we eat acts on the body. In Dr. Xiao's lab I fermented proso millet with three lactic acid bacteria and measured changes in phenolic profile, antioxidant activity, and starch digestibility.

    It was my first experience of a biological effect that only existed relative to a control, of replicate variability, and of an assay that had to be trusted before the number it produced could be. Two co-authored papers came out of that lab.

    What it left me with: measurement discipline, and the habit of asking what the assay is actually reporting.

  2. Understand · Complex systems and human variability

    2023 – 2026
    University of Nebraska–Lincoln · with Dr. Edward Deehan

    Clinical nutrition put food, microbes, metabolism, and human health inside one rigorous framework. As a master's student I coordinated a 12-week randomized crossover trial of dietary pulses and the gut microbiome: co-writing the protocol and consent, authoring SOPs, running more than 200 clinic visits, and handling stool, blood, blood-pressure, and dietary data from consent form to analysis.

    The gut microbiome taught me that phenotypes are produced by interacting communities, that the same intervention can produce different responders, and that context decides the outcome. A statistics minor and a 1,800-reference systematic review taught me to think in effect sizes, heterogeneity, and risk of bias rather than in single results. Validating machine-learning screening and a language model against human reviewers was my first computational assay with a control.

    What it left me with: design determines conclusions; confounding, selection, and adherence are real; data provenance is a scientific issue, not an administrative one; biological behaviour is interaction-driven.

Current · depth

Predict and design

  1. Predict · Modeling molecular recognition

    2026 – now
    Wang Lab · Dept. of Infectious Diseases & Immunology · with Dr. Yiquan Wang

    Running a trial and a systematic review made the limits of purely experimental and manual workflows concrete: time, scale, cost, and reproducibility. I decided that the questions I cared about needed quantitative modeling as well as measurement, and Dr. Wang's approach to AI-assisted biomedical research matched that conclusion.

    My current work is computational immunology: how antibody framework, binding loops, and epitope geometry interact in predicted antigen recognition, and when structure-prediction outputs are reliable enough to act on. A prediction is treated as an assay output, with native controls, replicates, and provenance.

    What I am building: structural intuition, pipeline engineering on an HPC cluster, and the habit of treating a model output as evidence with uncertainty.

  2. Design · From prediction to proposal

    developing

    The same project benchmarks generative antibody design tools against the controls used for prediction, asking which proposed molecules would be worth testing and by what criteria. Antibodies are my entry point rather than my boundary: a tractable system for learning to model, predict, and eventually design biological interactions.

    Where this points: closing the loop, so that designs return to experimental evidence and the evidence returns as new observations.

How I approach research

Six connected habits, one mindset

Not a checklist of separate values. Each habit exists because the others do: rigor needs validation, validation needs uncertainty, uncertainty needs context, and all of them serve the question.

One mindset Evidence first, whatever the tool

Experimental rigor

Controls, confounding, data provenance, reproducibility, validity. A computational assay earns trust the same way a wet one does.

Systems thinking

Biological behaviour emerges from interactions and context, not from one component ranked in isolation.

Cross-scale biology

Connect molecular interactions with systems-level behaviour and human relevance. State what a prediction means one scale up.

Question-first computation

Scientific questions determine methods. Statistics, structure prediction, clinical experiments, and wet-lab validation are all candidates.

Uncertainty-aware modeling

Predictions are evidence with uncertainty, not biological truth. Report distributions, confidence, and applicability domain.

Iterative validation

Computational prediction and biological evidence inform each other. Each pass through the loop sharpens the next question.
