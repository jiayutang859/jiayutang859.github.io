About

From the food lab to antibody design

I'm Jiayu Tang, an early-stage scientist interested in how biology, computation, and human health fit together.

I'm currently a Research Assistant in Dr. Yiquan Wang's lab at the University of Florida, and will soon begin my PhD in the same lab, building my research direction in computational antibody engineering, structural biology, and AI-driven protein design.

When I first tell people I studied food science, many assume it means culinary training. In reality, food science introduced me to chemistry, nutrition, microbiology, and the deeper question of how what we eat shapes human health.

That question has taken me through a few fields: from food science to nutrition, then the gut microbiome, then clinical trials, then AI for science, and now computational antibody engineering. Each step has felt less like a change of direction and more like zooming in on the same curiosity: how biological systems recognize and respond to the things they meet.

The path so far

How I got here

Five chapters, and the people who shaped them.

  1. Food science as a way into health

    2020 – 2023
    Northwest A&F University · with Dr. Chunxia Xiao

    Food science was my entry point into human health, nutrition, microbiology, and biological mechanisms. I became interested in how food, nutrients, and bioactive compounds influence the body.

    In Dr. Chunxia Xiao's lab I ran my first real experiments: fermenting proso millet with lactic acid bacteria and measuring how that changed its phenolic content, antioxidant activity, and starch digestibility. It was my first serious experience of experimental biomedical research, and it led to my first two co-authored papers.

  2. A 3+1 year that changed my direction

    2023 – 2024
    University of Nebraska–Lincoln · with Dr. Edward Deehan

    For the fourth year of my 3+1 dual-degree program I moved to the University of Nebraska–Lincoln. There I met Dr. Edward Deehan, whose work on human nutrition, the gut microbiome, and clinical trials deeply influenced me.

    Clinical nutrition connected everything I cared about (food, microbes, metabolism, and human health) inside a rigorous experimental framework.

  3. Learning rigor from clinical research

    2024 – 2026
    M.Sc., Food Science & Technology · Deehan Lab

    I stayed on for my master's, coordinating a 12-week randomized crossover trial on dietary pulses and the gut microbiome, and leading a systematic review that screened more than 1,800 references.

    Working with human participants taught me how much care, documentation, and patience good evidence requires, a lesson I now bring to computational work.

  4. Meeting AI for science

    During my master's

    The rise of generative AI and AI for science changed how I thought about biomedical research. I became convinced that future biomedical scientists need to understand not only biology and experiments, but also computation, AI-assisted workflows, and data-driven discovery.

    I started small: machine-learning screening tools and a large language model in my systematic review, checked carefully against human reviewers. I see AI not as a replacement for scientific judgment, but as a way to make science more creative, less repetitive, and more focused on meaningful biological questions.

  5. Computational antibody engineering

    2026 – now
    Wang Lab · Dept. of Infectious Diseases & Immunology · with Dr. Yiquan Wang

    That conviction led me to Dr. Yiquan Wang's lab at the University of Florida. I joined as a Research Assistant and will soon start my PhD there, building my research direction in computational antibody engineering, structural biology, and AI-driven protein design.

    My current work focuses on antibody frameworks, CDRs, epitope geometry, structure prediction, and molecular recognition. I'm early in this field and learning quickly. For me, antibodies are an entry point into AI for biology and biomedical design, not the only destination.

How I think about science

A few things I try to hold on to

These are working principles I'm still refining, not a finished philosophy.

Questions before tools

New AI models are exciting, but I try to start from a biological question worth answering and then choose tools that fit it, not the other way around.

Rigor travels

Protocols, controls, and careful documentation matter as much in a computational pipeline as they do in a clinic. Clinical research taught me that.

Learn across fields

Some of the best ideas come from people outside my own field. I try to learn in public, ask naive questions, and build with others.

Beyond research

Outside the lab

Philosophy keeps me asking why. Photography keeps me looking closely. And yes, I do love cooking, even though food science isn't culinary school.
