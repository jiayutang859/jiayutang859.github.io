AI-Designed Antibodies in 2026: The Hard Parts, the Pipelines, and the Path to Patients

What I learned trying to map the industry around my research: where AI drug discovery, and antibody design in particular, is still hard, how far it has really come, and what it would take for it to reach patients.

About 15 minutes to read · Sources checked 14 October 2026

I work on antibody design: using computation to propose antibodies and the bench to test them. Before going deeper, I wanted an honest map of the industry this work feeds into. Partly to understand where my research would land if it worked, partly to separate real progress from announcements, and partly, I admit, because reading about it makes me more excited to do the work. This is my attempt at that map, written in October 2026. It is a learner’s synthesis built from papers, filings and news, not investment advice, and I expect to revise it.

I want to answer six questions: where the hard problems are, what the newest methods can do, how far AI-made drugs have actually travelled, which milestones would mark real translation, how companies make money, and how patients benefit.

The process, and why the clinic dominates the math

The textbook path runs from target identification and validation, to finding hits, to optimizing a lead, through preclinical safety work, and then through three phases of clinical trials 1. What textbooks convey less vividly is how lopsided the risk is. Across 2011–2020, a program entering Phase I had a 7.9% chance of approval, and the average trip from Phase I to approval took 10.5 years; the hardest single step, Phase II to Phase III, was cleared only 28.9% of the time 2. Estimates of the cost per approved drug range from a median of about $1.1 billion based on public filings 3 to about $2.6 billion once the cost of capital and failures are included 4. And when clinical programs fail, the main reason is not chemistry: lack of efficacy accounts for 40–50% of failures and unmanageable toxicity for about 30%, while poor drug-like properties account for 10–15% 5.

Antibodies start from a better place. In the same dataset, monoclonal antibodies entering Phase I reached approval 12.1% of the time, against 7.5% for small molecules 2. That shapes how I read the field. An antibody’s specificity removes some of the off-target risk that sinks small molecules, so the remaining risk concentrates on two questions: is this the right target and epitope for the disease, and can this molecule be made, stored and dosed as a drug?

Today AI enters drug R&D at four points:

  • Target discovery. Mining disease, genetic and cellular data for mechanisms worth intervening in. What has to hold up: the association must be causal, not just correlated.
  • Molecule design. Searching for or generating candidates: small molecules, antibodies, other proteins. My own work sits here.
  • Lead optimization. Improving activity, selectivity, toxicity, pharmacokinetics and developability: the stage where “it binds” has to become “it is a good drug.”
  • Clinical development. Helping with patient stratification, trial design and analysis. Here model performance cannot stand in for evidence of efficacy and safety in people.

The early clinical evidence fits this picture. In the first systematic look at molecules from AI-native companies, 80–90% passed Phase I, well above historical averages, but about 40% passed Phase II, roughly the same as everyone else 6. Phase I mostly tests safety. My reading is that AI is already good at producing molecules that behave, and not yet proven at choosing biology that works.

Where antibody design is hard

For antibodies specifically, I see five hard problems, and they sit at different stages of the process.

1. Choosing the epitope, not just the target

A target is a protein. An epitope is the patch on it that an antibody touches, and that patch decides whether binding blocks a function, activates one, or does nothing. The hardest case is an epitope with no known antibody or protein partner at all. Absci’s Origin-1 study took on exactly this case: across 10 such targets, with fewer than 100 designs each, it obtained developable, specific antibodies for four 7. Even the strongest platforms report honest failures. Chai Discovery tested 48 and 50 designs against two peptide–MHC targets and found no binders, while a third target yielded two 8.

2. Predicting the interface

Antibody binding is concentrated in the CDR loops, and the most variable of them, CDR-H3, is still the hardest to model: a specialized predictor reaches about 2.8 Å RMSD on CDR-H3, barely better than AlphaFold-Multimer 9. For whole antibody–antigen complexes, AlphaFold 3 was a significant step forward, but its authors ranked antibody predictions across 1,000 model seeds instead of the usual five, and noted that the gain from more seeds was specific to antibodies 10. In an independent benchmark of recently released complexes, even the best general model produced an acceptable antibody–antigen interface less than half the time (47.9% for AlphaFold 3) 11.

3. Data that is large in the wrong places

The Observed Antibody Space holds more than a billion antibody sequences 12, but sequences record what immune systems made, not what binds what, or how tightly. Structural data is far smaller: in October 2026 the Structural Antibody Database listed 11,664 antibody-containing PDB entries 13. Affinity data is smaller still. A 2025 study found that models predicting how mutations change antibody–antigen binding overfit the few hundred experimental measurements available, and concluded that orders of magnitude more data, and more diverse data, would be needed to generalize 14.

4. Developability, the multi-objective middle

A binder is not a drug. It also has to express well, stay stable, avoid sticking to the wrong things, resist aggregation, remain injectable at high concentration, and not provoke an immune response. When 137 clinical-stage antibodies were run through 12 biophysical assays, the measures of specificity, non-specific binding and self-association, best separated approved antibodies from those still in trials 15. Computational guidelines distilled from clinical antibodies exist 16, but a 2025 open competition on predicting developability concluded, in its own title, that model performance was limited and that the field needs standardized data 17. A 2026 review frames the whole task as multi-objective optimization 18. Improving one property often costs another, which is why I see developability as the real center of the problem rather than a final filter.

5. Efficacy in people

The last hard problem is not computational at all. Whether engaging a target changes a disease in a given population is decided in Phase II and Phase III trials. That is where most programs fail 5, and where AI-derived molecules have so far done about as well as everyone else 6.

What the newest methods can do

The last two years changed what “AI antibody design” means. Instead of screening millions of variants, groups now test tens of designs per target and report hit rates.

  • De novo design to a chosen epitope. The Baker lab fine-tuned RFdiffusion to design single-domain antibodies, scFvs and full antibodies against specified epitopes. Cryo-EM confirmed the designed binding poses, and initial affinities in the tens to hundreds of nanomolar were matured to single-digit nanomolar 19.
  • Zero-shot hit rates. Chai-2 reported a 16% hit rate across 52 targets, testing at most 20 designs per target, with at least one binder for half of them 20. A follow-up designed full-length IgGs, more than 86% of which had strong developability profiles, and produced binders to six GPCRs, including functional agonists for two 8. Both are company preprints, not yet peer-reviewed.
  • Open methods. Germinal, now published in Nature Biotechnology, reported experimental success rates of 4–22% across four targets in its preprint, testing only 43–101 designs per target 21.
  • Novel epitopes. In Origin-1, cryo-EM structures closely matched the designed complexes (DockQ 0.73–0.83) 7.
  • Lab-in-the-loop optimization. Genentech’s Prescient Design ran generative models, property predictors and wet-lab testing in a semi-autonomous loop: more than 1,800 variants across four targets, 3–100-fold binding improvements for every target, and best binders around 100 pM 22.

The most useful counterweight is a blinded benchmark. AIntibody, a CASP-style challenge, evaluated 511 AI-designed or AI-predicted antibodies from 29 organizations. Several groups produced developable antibodies with affinities below 100 pM, but those successes did not transfer across tasks, and, with one exception, choosing high-affinity clones by model prediction did worse than choosing them at random 23.

My summary: generating binders is becoming tractable. Ranking them, guaranteeing specificity and developability, and predicting function are not yet.

Three things stand out to me. First, the most advanced AI antibody, GB-0895, was optimized by AI rather than designed from scratch, and its selling point is a drug-like property rather than a new target. It binds TSLP at 106 fM, about 20 times tighter than the approved antibody tezepelumab, and after a single dose its mean half-life was about 98 days, which supports dosing every six months 30. Rentosertib, the furthest-advanced small molecule with both an AI-derived target and an AI-generated molecule, earned its Phase 3 on a small 12-week Phase 2a trial 31.

Second, timelines slip and pipelines get pruned. Isomorphic Labs moved its expected first clinical trials from the end of 2025 to the end of 2026 32. Recursion cut programs and about 20% of its staff in 2025 33. Schrödinger said in November 2025 that it would no longer take discovery programs into the clinic on its own 34, and Absci decided not to run later-stage development of its first antibody, ABS-101, itself 35.

Third, no antibody designed fully from scratch has entered a human trial yet. That will be a symbolic moment, but a Phase 1 trial mostly tests safety; the real test is the efficacy readout that follows.

The milestones I am watching

If I ask what would show that AI design has truly translated, rather than produced promising preprints, I arrive at six milestones, roughly in order:

  1. Blinded wins over standard discovery. Prospective benchmarks in which AI design consistently beats library selection across tasks, not just in one regime. AIntibody shows we are not there yet 23.
  2. Developability by design. Designs that arrive drug-like without rounds of rescue engineering, measured on shared, standardized assays 17.
  3. A de novo antibody in humans. The first antibody designed zero-shot, without immunization or library screening, dosed in a Phase 1 trial.
  4. Phase II success above the historical rate. For me, this is the decisive one. AI-derived molecules currently pass Phase II at about the historical rate 6. A sustained improvement would mean AI is helping choose biology that works, not just molecules that behave. A near-term readout to watch is ABS-201’s interim proof-of-concept data, expected in the second half of 2026 27.
  5. A first approval. Zasocitinib’s FDA decision, expected in the first quarter of 2027, would be a landmark for computational design generally 24. GB-0895’s Phase 3 trials, with primary completion listed around the end of 2028, could make an AI-optimized antibody next 36.
  6. Regulators accepting computational evidence. This is already moving. In April 2025 the FDA announced a plan to phase out animal-testing requirements, starting with monoclonal antibodies and naming AI-based computational models of toxicity among the replacements 37. In December 2025 it issued draft guidance to reduce non-human primate studies for antibodies, noting that a typical program uses more than 100 primates at about $50,000 each 38. In January 2026 the FDA and EMA published shared principles for good AI practice in drug development 39. The FDA’s broader guidance on AI for regulatory decisions, however, is still a draft 40.

How the companies make money

One company may cover several stages. What decides its future is how it turns capability into revenue, and I see three models. Most companies blend them.

Selling tools. Customers pay for software or platform access. Schrödinger is the reference case: in 2025 it earned $199.5M from software and $56.4M from drug discovery, on total revenue of $255.9M, with a net loss of $103.3M 41. Its largest windfall came not from software but from its equity in Nimbus, whose TYK2 program Takeda bought for $4 billion upfront 42. Newer antibody-design companies also look partly like tool sellers. Chai Discovery has announced partnerships with Lilly, Pfizer and Novartis, among others, and raised a $400M Series C 43. The risk in this model is that the largest customers can build their own: Lilly has opened its TuneLab models to biotechs 44 and committed up to $1 billion over five years to an AI co-innovation lab with NVIDIA 45.

Discovering for partners. A pharmaceutical partner pays an upfront fee, then milestone payments as a program advances, then royalties if a drug sells. The headline numbers are mostly contingent. Isomorphic Labs’ 2024 deals with Lilly and Novartis carried $82.5M upfront against about $2.9 billion in potential milestones 46. Recursion says it has received more than $500M in upfront and milestone payments since its founding, against partnership headlines worth many billions 47. By its IPO, Generate had received about $110M from Amgen and Novartis against roughly $3 billion in announced deal value 48. Across life-science acquisitions since 2008, only about 9.5% of potential milestone payments were actually paid 49. When I read “up to $X billion,” I now look for the upfront first.

Owning the pipeline. Here the value depends on the drugs: which disease, what stage, how strong the evidence, and whether the company can fund itself to the next readout. It is the highest-reward model and the most expensive. Recursion reported a 2025 net loss of $644.8M on revenue of $74.7M 47. The clearest recent pivot is Generate, which raised $400M in a February 2026 IPO and now leads with its Phase 3 antibody 48,26.

My conclusion is that the market pays most where clinical risk has been removed. A platform ultimately proves its value through an asset, so even tool companies end up judged by the molecules they help create.

How patients benefit

“Faster and cheaper discovery” is how AI is usually sold. I think the benefit to patients is more specific than that.

Better molecules, fewer injections. Engineering antibody properties has already changed treatment without AI. Ravulizumab is given every 8 weeks instead of every 2 weeks for its predecessor, eculizumab 50. Nirsevimab protects an infant through an entire RSV season with a single dose 51. Subcutaneous pembrolizumab takes 1–2 minutes to inject instead of a 30-minute infusion 52. If AI makes properties like these designable from the start, as GB-0895’s six-month dosing aims to 30, that is a direct benefit to patients.

New targets. Membrane proteins such as GPCRs have been hard to reach with antibodies. Designed binders and agonists against them 8 could open mechanisms that were previously out of reach.

Fewer animals and faster safety work. Validated computational and cell-based methods could reduce primate studies for antibodies 38.

Prices are a separate question. I was surprised that lower R&D costs do not automatically lead to lower prices. Across 60 drugs approved from 2009 to 2018, R&D investment was not associated with list or net prices 53. Prices fall through competition and policy instead. The FDA had licensed 89 biosimilars by August 2026 54, and the first Medicare-negotiated prices, effective January 2026, included the antibody ustekinumab (Stelara) at $4,695 against a $13,836 list price for 30 days 55. So when I imagine patients benefiting from AI, I think first of better and more convenient drugs for diseases that lacked them, and only second of cheaper ones.

What this means for me

Writing this changed how I think about my own training. The bottleneck is moving from “can we generate a binder?” to “which binder, at which epitope, with which properties, changes an outcome in people?” That is close to the question in my research philosophy: how an interaction, in its context, produces an outcome. It also tells me which skills will matter. Computational design that is honest about uncertainty. Wet-lab validation that is prospective and quantitative. Developability treated as a design objective rather than a late filter. And an understanding of clinical evidence, which my earlier work in human clinical research gave me a start on.

The field is moving quickly, and several of the readouts above are due within months. I will update these notes as they arrive.
