What AI Is, and How I Use It: Thoughts on Terence Tao’s “Slightly Drunk Collaborator”

Three years of working with AI, the trap of letting it think for me, and the rules I now try to follow.

Terence Tao once described working with AI as “having a collaborator who knows a lot, is slightly drunk, and is just throwing out random ideas that do not make any sense.” I keep coming back to that line, because it describes my own experience better than most announcements do. We are told almost weekly that OpenAI, Anthropic and others have cracked another conjecture or problem in some field, and it is easy to conclude that AI has simply become “smarter.” I am not sure that is the right reading. In October 2025, OpenAI researchers announced that GPT-5 had solved several open Erdős problems; the maintainer of the problem database pointed out that the model had in fact found existing papers that already solved them, and the claim was walked back. That is genuinely useful work, but it is a different thing from insight. The collaborator knows a lot. Knowing a lot is not the same as knowing where to go.

I have used AI for about three years, and my relationship with it has moved through stages: shock, then delight at how fast and easy everything felt, then worry, anxiety, and a quiet loss of meaning, and finally an effort to understand what it actually is. At first it wrote my emails, built my schedules and summarized papers. When coding agents arrived, it wrote small programs and tools for me. Then it became a partner for brainstorming, drafting documents, and searching for and organizing information. Now it writes analysis code during my experiments, manages my files and automates parts of my dry-lab work. The tools improve almost daily, yet I have not felt lighter.

When I started running agents in auto mode, I slipped into not wanting to think. I accepted recommendation after recommendation, and more than once I ended up with data and methods that ran directly against what I had set out to test, and that meant nothing. That was when it became clear to me:

AI will do a hundred things for you automatically, and only one or two of them are actually useful.

So, from Tao and from fragments I have gathered elsewhere, I have tried to write down how I want to work. The first rule is no push-button autonomy. I want a human–AI hybrid organized like a modular production line. Complex work should be broken into many small, independent sub-problems with clear interfaces, the way a mathematician breaks a proof into lemmas. The human designs the architecture, chooses the route and defines the constraints. AI generates or helps complete the individual modules in bulk, and the human integrates and checks the result.

Within that line, AI can play the role of proposing hypotheses and planning experiments, or the role of executing them, but never both at once. When the same system both decides what to ask and carries out the answer, nobody is left to notice when the question has quietly changed. A human has to stay in the loop, because a sense of direction is something only the human brings. AI can produce a hundred ideas without effort, but quantity is not quality, and choosing among them is the work.

That is why I think the most important role in any hybrid is still the thinker. AI can replace my repetitive labor. It should not replace my thinking, my choices, my values or my sense of meaning. I suspect the habit of handing these over started before generative AI, with recommendation algorithms on social media. They chose for me: funny short videos that made me happy, cooking techniques I wanted to learn, life hacks, things to buy. I no longer had to search for what interested me; I only had to accept what was offered. After years of that, I noticed I had become lazy, both in how I acted and in how I thought.

A slightly drunk collaborator who knows a lot can be wonderful company, as long as someone sober decides where we are going. I want to keep being that person.
