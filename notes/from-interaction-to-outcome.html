Research philosophy · October 2026

From Interaction to Outcome

Functional foods, the gut microbiome, human clinical trials, and now antibodies: the subjects changed, but one question kept returning. Why does an interaction produce one outcome rather than another, and can we change it?

My research has moved through functional foods, the gut microbiome, human clinical trials, and now computational immunology and antibody engineering. On paper, these can look like changes of direction. For a long time, I thought about them that way too. But looking back, I have realized that the biological object itself was rarely what kept me interested. Across these different systems, I kept returning to a similar question: why does an interaction produce one outcome rather than another?

I first encountered this question through food. A seemingly simple question (why are certain foods beneficial to human health?) quickly becomes difficult once we ask what “beneficial” actually means. A food contains many components, those components encounter a complex gut microbial community, microbes transform them into other molecules, and those changes occur inside a host with a particular physiology, genetics, lifestyle, and environment. During my work with the gut microbiome and human clinical research, an even more interesting question emerged: why can two people receive the same dietary intervention and respond differently? At that point, the food itself was no longer enough to explain the outcome. The interaction between intervention, microbiome, host, and context became part of the explanation.

Years later, I find myself asking surprisingly similar questions in immunology. Why can people infected by the same virus develop very different immune responses? At a much smaller scale, why does one antibody recognize an antigen successfully while another does not? Why can changing the framework surrounding the same antigen-recognizing regions alter the predicted behavior of an antibody? The objects have changed, and the scale has changed dramatically, but the structure of the question feels familiar.

I have gradually come to think about many of these problems through a simple abstraction:

A and B may be a dietary component and a microorganism, a microbial community and its host, an antigen and an antibody, or more complicated collections of biological entities. The context may be molecular structure, environment, physiology, genetics, or the state of the larger biological system. Understanding A and B and how they interact is essential, but I have realized that what interests me most is C.

What is C? Why does C emerge? Can we predict C? Can we change C?

This distinction has gradually reshaped how I think about research. An antibody binding an antigen is an interaction; neutralization is an outcome, an altered immune response is another, and a clinical effect lies further downstream. A dietary molecule being metabolized by a bacterium is an interaction; changes in microbial ecology, host physiology, and eventually health are outcomes at progressively higher levels. The scientific challenge that fascinates me is therefore not simply identifying interactions, but tracing how interactions and context give rise to outcomes across these levels.

It also shapes what I want from computation and AI. I am excited by our growing ability to predict biological behavior, but prediction alone does not completely satisfy me. A black-box model may tell us that C will happen without telling us why. I am more interested in causal and interpretable prediction: identifying which factors actually drive C, how they work together, and whether those explanations survive experimental testing. To me, prediction is partly a test of understanding. If we believe we understand why an outcome occurs, that understanding should eventually help us anticipate what will happen in a new system.

And if we understand C well enough to predict it, the next question becomes almost unavoidable: can we move it? If a particular molecular feature, microbial state, environmental context, or biological mechanism causally contributes to an outcome, it becomes more than an explanation; it becomes a possible point of intervention. We might redesign a molecule, alter an interaction, change the context, or choose an intervention appropriate for a particular biological state. This is where my interest in interaction connects to causality, and where causality connects to translation.

My research philosophy, in practice

To understand, predict, and modulate biological interactions in humans.

Not one biological object, but one reusable way of working: a five-stage cycle that takes an interaction from observation to a changed outcome, and that can be applied at any scale.

  2. 02 · Understand

  3. 03 · Model & predict

Same cycle, different scales.
  • antibody · antigen
  • molecule · cell
  • diet · gut microbiome · host
  • intervention · human physiology

I now see my research trajectory as a movement through interaction → causality → translation. My earlier work exposed me to the complexity of real human outcomes, where the same intervention does not necessarily produce the same response. My current work with antibodies takes me toward the other end of the scale, where interactions can be decomposed into structures, sequences, models, hypotheses, and experimentally testable perturbations. Rather than seeing these experiences as disconnected, I increasingly see them as complementary: one taught me how complicated C can be; the other is teaching me how to take C apart.

I do not yet have a universal framework for connecting molecules to human outcomes, and I do not expect biology to collapse into a simple equation. But this is the direction in which I want to grow as a scientist. I want to understand why biological exposures and interventions produce different outcomes, identify the interactions and causal determinants behind that variation, build models that can predict it, and ultimately use that knowledge to design interventions.

The biological objects I study may change. The question I want to keep asking is much more stable:

What creates C, and can understanding C well enough eventually allow us to change it in ways that benefit human health?
